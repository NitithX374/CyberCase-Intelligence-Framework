\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) can summarise cybercrime complaints that span several documents, but an
investigator cannot act on a summary without knowing which sentence of which document supports each
statement, and citations written by the model are as fragile as the rest of its output. We present a
method that makes evidence traceability a property of the system rather than a request in the
prompt. Every source is cut into contiguous Source units that reconstruct it exactly; each unit gets
a content-addressed identifier tied to the exact text it came from; the model sees only short
aliases and may \emph{select} them but never write a quotation; and a deterministic binding step
resolves each selection to a character range and page, or records it as an invalid pointer. Summary
sentences end with the identifiers of the claims they rest on, giving a two-level provenance chain
from sentence to claim to verbatim span. On public Thai and English text, units reconstruct every
source exactly, a one-character edit invalidates every old identifier, and unit boundaries fall inside a
word in under 0.5\% of cuts against 65--75\% for fixed-size chunks. As an optional layer, a frozen NLI-based check labels each
claim with a warning. On WiCE it improves Macro-F1 over thresholded NLI by 0.033; machine
translation into Thai raises its false acceptance by 17.4 points; and used as a hard filter it cut
unsupported claims cited in summaries from 100\% to 22.2\% while withholding 31\% of supported ones,
which is why it only warns.

\keywords{Evidence traceability \and Provenance \and Large language models \and
Attributed generation \and Cybercrime investigation \and Low-resource languages}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%======================================================================

Cybercrime complaints rarely arrive as one clean narrative. A typical case holds a victim's
account, screenshots of chats, bank transfer slips and scanned police forms, much of it in Thai
and much of it recovered by optical character recognition (OCR). LLMs can condense such a case
into a readable summary, but they also produce fluent statements that no source supports
\cite{ji2023hallucination}. In an investigation, an unsupported amount, date or name is not a
stylistic flaw: it can send an officer after the wrong account, or put a wrong figure into a
request to freeze funds.

The common remedy is to make the model cite: attributed-generation methods train or
prompt models to attach citations or quoted spans to each sentence
\cite{gao2023alce,slobodkin2024afg}. Domain workflows go further and label every output unit
with an explicit evidence identifier \cite{yang2026traceable}. In all of these, however, the
pointer is still text written by the model, so its existence and accuracy must be checked
afterwards, often by people. A wrong citation looks exactly like a correct one to a busy reader.

Thai, a low-resource language for this task, adds two difficulties. Sentence boundaries are not marked by punctuation, so even deciding
what to quote requires a segmenter \cite{phatthiyaphaibun2023pythainlp}; and most claim
verification resources and checkpoints are English. The multilingual NLI checkpoint we use, for
example, was deliberately trained without Thai NLI data \cite{laurer2022mdeberta}. Any semantic
check a Thai system relies on is therefore a cross-lingual transfer whose behaviour has to be
measured, not assumed.

We take a different position: \emph{the model chooses evidence; the system owns it}.
The model never writes a quotation, an offset or a page number. It may only select identifiers
that the system issued for spans it can reproduce byte for byte, and everything the user sees as
evidence is regenerated from storage. Our main contribution is the method that makes this
possible, described step by step with a worked Thai example:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Lossless, Thai-aware Source units}: a segmentation procedure that cuts every source into
contiguous units with exact offsets that reconstruct it character for character
(Sect.~\ref{sec:units}, Fig.~\ref{fig:units}), checked on public Thai and English text (Sect.~\ref{sec:props}).
\item \emph{Content-addressed identifiers and request-scoped aliases}: unit identifiers bound to the
exact source text, and short aliases for the model whose mapping is fixed before the model is called
(Sects.~\ref{sec:ids}--\ref{sec:reading}).
\item \emph{Deterministic binding with a two-level provenance chain}: every selection is resolved or
recorded with a typed failure reason, and every summary sentence leads to claims (why) and from claims
to verbatim spans with page locators (where) (Sect.~\ref{sec:binding}, Fig.~\ref{fig:provenance}).
\end{enumerate}
As a secondary contribution, we evaluate an optional semantic warning layer on English and
machine-translated Thai benchmarks and in a paired downstream replay, and show why it should warn
rather than filter (Sects.~\ref{sec:warning} and~\ref{sec:eval}).
The setting is a public-safety service where trustworthiness has to be inspectable: every
statement an officer reads should lead back to a document, a page and a character range.

\section{Related Work}
%======================================================================

Two lines of work meet in this paper: traceability, which asks whether an output can be followed
back to what produced it, and attribute-first generation, which makes evidence selection an explicit
step before writing. We also draw on claim verification for the semantic check.

\subsection{Traceability and Provenance}

Traceability has a long history outside NLP. In requirements engineering it is the ability to
follow the life of a requirement both forwards and backwards; Gotel and Finkelstein argued that many
problems stem from missing information about where an artifact came from and how it was produced
\cite{gotel1994traceability}. Database research distinguishes \emph{why}-provenance, the
source records that justify an output, from \emph{where}-provenance, the exact location a value was
copied from \cite{buneman2001provenance}. Our design maps directly onto this vocabulary:
a citation is where-provenance (a source and a character range), the claim identifiers on a summary
sentence are why-provenance, and both are recorded when the output is produced rather than
reconstructed afterwards.

For LLM output, the need is well documented. An audit of four generative search engines found that
only 51.5\% of generated sentences were fully supported by their citations and only 74.5\% of
citations supported the sentence they were attached to \cite{liu2023verifiability}. In medicine,
between 50\% and 90\% of LLM responses were not fully supported by the sources they cited
\cite{wu2025sourcecheckup}. Domain
systems respond with explicit evidence records. Yang et al.\ register exhibition evidence under
stable identifiers and label each generated unit as grounded or creative, auditing the links
manually \cite{yang2026traceable}; Dehing et al.\ carry message trace identifiers into structured
forensic reports \cite{dehing2026forensics}; Worledge et al.\ locate every quote on its court-file
page \cite{worledge2026default}. In each case the identifier or quote is written by the model and
checked afterwards; in ours it is issued and resolved by the system.

\subsection{Attribute First, then Generate}

Attribution is commonly judged by whether a statement is supported by an identified source
\cite{rashkin2023ais}, and support alone is not enough: up to 57\% of citations in a recent study were
post-rationalised rather than the evidence the model actually used \cite{wallat2025faithfulness}. Two families of methods followed.
\emph{Post-hoc} methods generate first and attach or repair evidence later: RARR searches for
evidence for an existing output and revises unsupported content \cite{gao2023rarr}, and ALCE
benchmarks models that cite retrieved passages while writing \cite{gao2023alce}. \emph{Attribute-first}
methods invert the order. Slobodkin et al.\ select concise source spans, cluster them into a
sentence-level plan, and only then generate, discarding selected spans that cannot be found in the
source \cite{slobodkin2024afg}; this shortened human verification time in both of their tasks,
while human-judged attribution rose in one task and fell in the other.

Later work refines the selected unit and what is done with it. FRONT trains models to extract
fine-grained quotes before writing grounded citations \cite{huang2024front}; LongCite trains long-context models to cite
at sentence level as they write \cite{zhang2025longcite}; GenerationPrograms
expresses the answer as an executable program of operations over source sentences, so that each
output sentence carries its derivation \cite{wan2025generationprograms}; Worledge et al.\ show
that moving from quotation toward abstraction makes answers more fluent and useful but harder and
slower to verify \cite{worledge2024spectrum}; CiteLab provides tooling to compare citation workflows,
attribute-first among them \cite{shen2025citelab}; and QA-based propositions can localise exactly
which part of an attributed sentence is inconsistent with its source \cite{cattan2026localizing}.
It has been applied to legal review \cite{worledge2026default} and used as a medical baseline
\cite{wu2025medgraphrag}; related work moves the evidence unit to page regions \cite{zhao2025finragbenchv}
or filters training data for attributability \cite{schimanski2024specialists}.

We keep the attribute-first order but change what is selected. Our reader model selects opaque identifiers
issued by the system instead of copying spans, so the existence check that attribute-first methods
perform by string matching becomes an exact lookup, and a quotation can never drift from its source.

\subsection{Claim Verification}

A third line checks generated text against evidence. FActScore checks atomic facts
\cite{min2023factscore},
MiniCheck trains small grounding checkers \cite{tang2024minicheck}, and AttrScore judges attribution
with LLMs and fine-tuned models \cite{yue2023attrscore}; WiCE \cite{kamoi2023wice} and
AttributionBench \cite{li2024attributionbench} supply labelled claim/evidence pairs. For Thai, XNLI
provides professionally translated NLI test data \cite{conneau2018xnli}, and PyThaiNLP provides
sentence segmentation \cite{phatthiyaphaibun2023pythainlp}. AUTOSUMM, deployed for bank call summaries, combines entity, embedding and entailment layers and
shows sentence-level flags to reviewers \cite{gupta2025autosumm}, close to our warning layer but without
evidence pointers. Table~\ref{tab:compare} compares these systems. We do not propose a new checker: we
assemble a cheap, frozen one from multilingual components \cite{reimers2020multilingual} and measure
what it does at one point in the pipeline.

\section{Problem Setting and Requirements}\label{sec:problem}
%======================================================================

Table~\ref{tab:notation} lists the notation. A case $K$ owns sources $S$ (typed narratives or text
extracted from documents, with page locators). $M_R$ reads them and writes claims $C$, each with
evidence lists $E^{+}(c)$ and $E^{-}(c)$; $M_J$ writes a summary $\Sigma$ whose sentences $\sigma$
name the claims $R(\sigma)$ they rest on.

From the task itself (an investigator must be able to justify each statement in a case file) and
from earlier versions of our system in which the model wrote its own quotations, which then had to be
matched back to the sources approximately, we derived five requirements. Evidence must be
verbatim: every quotation shown is a substring of a stored source, reproduced by the system and never
by a model. Failures must be visible: a pointer that cannot be resolved is shown as invalid, not dropped
or repaired. Pointers must be safe across revisions: one issued for a version of a document never binds
to another. Provenance must reach the sentence: every summary sentence names the claims it rests on, and
only claims in $C$. Finally, cost must stay bounded: expensive stages run only when the case is complete
enough, and any semantic check is cheap enough to run on every claim. The first four are structural and
can be enforced by construction; the last shapes the pipeline. Whether a
claim is \emph{true}, or even entailed by its citations, is a separate question that no structural
rule answers, which is what the warning layer of Sect.~\ref{sec:check} addresses, imperfectly.

\subsection{Overview}
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows where the method sits: PDF pages are read by OCR so page locators are
known, and $M_G$ checks the case against a 5W1H checklist and either asks the user one question and
stops, or proceeds, so the expensive stages run only on cases complete enough to analyse. The four
steps below make the evidence traceable.

\subsection{Step 1: Lossless Source Units}\label{sec:units}
Thai writes no sentence-final punctuation, so the boundaries of a quotable span have to be computed.
Fig.~\ref{fig:units}A shows the procedure on a 157-character narrative. (1) $S$ is split into lines,
keeping their offsets, and each non-empty line into sentences with the multilingual SaT segmenter
\cite{frohmann2024sat}, falling back to PyThaiNLP's CRF segmenter \cite{phatthiyaphaibun2023pythainlp}
when SaT is unavailable; if the pieces do not concatenate back to the line exactly, the whole line is
kept as one piece. (2) Pieces shorter than $\delta$ non-space characters are merged into the following
piece, so that interjections such as \emph{tok jai mak} (``I was shocked'', Fig.~\ref{fig:units}) do not become citable units on
their own. (3) $S$ is then cut at every piece start, at the start of every table and table row produced
by OCR, and every $L$ characters within long pieces. Each unit runs from its start to the next unit's
start, so whitespace and line breaks belong to a unit rather than falling between units. Unlike
fixed-size chunks, which can split a Thai word from the number it governs (Fig.~\ref{fig:units}B), units
follow sentence boundaries.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/units.pdf}
\caption{Building Source units for a constructed Thai narrative. (A) Segmentation into contiguous
units with exact offsets; a 7-character interjection is merged into the next piece. (B) For contrast,
fixed 40-character chunks of the same text split the word for ``transfer'' from the amount, while units
keep whole sentences. (C) Identifiers are tied to the exact text, so one edited digit makes the old
identifier stale. The sentence split is illustrative; offsets and hashes are computed with the
production rules.}
\label{fig:units}
\end{figure}

This construction gives two guarantees. First, the units partition $S$ without loss: for $U(S)=(u_1,\dots,u_n)$,
$u_k.\mathit{text} = S[u_k.\mathit{start}{:}u_k.\mathit{end}]$, $u_1.\mathit{start}=0$,
$u_k.\mathit{end}=u_{k+1}.\mathit{start}$ and $u_n.\mathit{end}=|S|$, so concatenating the units returns
$S$. Second, segmentation is deterministic: $U(S)$ is a pure function of $S$ and $v$, so units are
recomputed on demand rather than stored.

\subsection{Step 2: Content-Addressed Identifiers}\label{sec:ids}
Each unit is named by its source, its position $k$ and a revision tag computed from the whole source:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:id}
\mathit{id}(u_k) = S.\mathit{id}\,\texttt{:U}\,k\,\texttt{-}\,h_S, \qquad
h_S = \mathrm{SHA\text{-}256}\bigl(v \,\|\, \texttt{\textbackslash 0} \,\|\, S\bigr)_{[0:16]},
\end{equation}
where $k$ is zero-padded to three digits and $v=\texttt{evidence\_units\_v3}$. In Fig.~\ref{fig:units}C,
$h_{S1}=\texttt{80a1d348f2908e99}$, and changing 4,500 to 4,800 gives \texttt{660d3b84ad7f6992}. Identifiers are therefore safe across revisions: editing any
character of $S$ or changing $v$ changes $h_S$, so an identifier issued for an earlier text cannot
resolve against the current one; a different text would need a 64-bit prefix collision of SHA-256 to reuse an identifier.

\subsection{Step 3: Request-Scoped Aliases and Claims-Only Reading}\label{sec:reading}
Long identifiers are costly to copy and easy to garble, so $M_R$ sees each unit only as its alias
$\alpha(u)$ (\texttt{U001}, \texttt{U002}, \dots) with its text, together with the source's document
and extraction-quality metadata; offsets and hashes are never shown. Before $M_R$ is called, the request
records $S.\mathit{id}\mapsto h_S$ for every source and later expands each selected alias with
\emph{that} revision, so a selection is always expanded against the exact text $M_R$ was shown.

$M_R$ returns a strict JSON object containing only $C$. Each $c\in C$ has an identifier, a type, its
text, an epistemic status (\texttt{reported} for attributed assertions, \texttt{unknown} for uncertainty
stated in a source) and the alias lists $E^{+}(c)$ and $E^{-}(c)$ per source. $M_R$ cannot emit
quotations, offsets, reasoning or separate lists of parties and events; inference beyond the sources is
left to $M_J$. Every model reply is validated against a strict local schema and retried at most once.

\subsection{Step 4: Deterministic Binding and the Provenance Chain}\label{sec:binding}
$\beta$ checks each expanded identifier in a fixed order and records the first failing check as its
reason $\rho$: the source is not in $K$ (\texttt{unknown\_source}); the identifier is malformed
(\texttt{malformed\_id}); it names another source (\texttt{cross\_source}); its revision differs from
the recorded one (\texttt{stale\_id}); no such unit exists (\texttt{unknown\_unit}); or the unit is only
whitespace (\texttt{empty\_unit}). Independently, an alias already cited by the same $c$ gives
\texttt{duplicate\_id}. A result $\bot_\rho$ is stored and shown as an invalid pointer, never repaired or
dropped; otherwise $\beta$ returns the citation $(S,u.\mathit{start},u.\mathit{end},\mathit{page})$
and the system reproduces $u.\mathit{text}$. Because every quotation in the analysis view and the
report is produced this way, it is a substring of a stored source by construction.

$M_J$ then writes $\Sigma$ from $C$ alone, ending each $\sigma$ with the bracketed identifiers of
$R(\sigma)$, and a separate call extracts parties, timeline and impacts from $C$ for display.
References are closed as well: if any $R(\sigma)$ or gap names a claim outside $C$, the analysis
fails and is not stored. The result is the chain in Fig.~\ref{fig:provenance}: $\sigma\to R(\sigma)$ is
why-provenance in the sense of \cite{buneman2001provenance}, and $c\to\beta(E^{+}(c))$ is
where-provenance. A sentence is \emph{bound} when every $c\in R(\sigma)$ has resolved support,
\emph{mixed} when some do, \emph{unbound} when none do and \emph{no\_claim} when $R(\sigma)=\emptyset$;
these states describe addressability, not truth. Reports are deterministic snapshots of this chain and
call no model.

\subsection{Optional Semantic Warning Layer}\label{sec:warning}\label{sec:check}
Binding guarantees that evidence exists and is quoted exactly; it says nothing about whether it supports
the claim. As an optional layer, each bound $c$ with status \texttt{reported} is checked by B1-LR: a
frozen multilingual MPNet encoder \cite{reimers2020multilingual} keeps the units of $\beta(E^{+}(c))$ with
cosine similarity $\ge 0.20$ to $c$ (the most similar one if none pass); a multilingual mDeBERTa-v3 NLI
model \cite{he2021debertav3,laurer2022mdeberta} scores their concatenation against $c$; and a logistic
regression fitted once on the WiCE training split \cite{kamoi2023wice} maps the probabilities to
$s(c)=\mathrm{sig}(1.411\,p_E-1.051\,p_N-0.302\,p_C+0.058)$, where $\mathrm{sig}$ is the logistic function, giving $\mathrm{w}(c)=\texttt{supported}$ when
$s(c)\ge 0.5$ and \texttt{not\_supported} otherwise. $\mathrm{w}(c)$ is shown beside $c$; it never removes
a claim, never enters a model prompt, and missing model files give \texttt{unassessed} without failing
the analysis. The prototype uses Gemma~4~26B for $M_G$, $M_R$ and $M_J$; the warning-layer models run
locally.

\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}
%======================================================================

We first check that the construction behaves on real text as Sect.~\ref{sec:system} claims, and then
measure the optional warning layer, whose errors are statistical. For the warning layer we ask whether
the B1-LR rule improves on thresholded NLI, how it behaves when its inputs are machine-translated into
Thai, and what happens downstream if its verdict is used to filter claims instead of labelling them. No
component was
trained or tuned on test data, and we report every outcome of the agreed runs, including failures.

\subsection{The Construction on Public Text}\label{sec:props}

The guarantees of Sect.~\ref{sec:system} follow from the code, but a segmenter can still misbehave on
real text. We ran the production code, with SaT and no model call, on 500 Thai news articles from the
ThaiSum test set \cite{chumpolsathien2020thaisum} and 1,000 English references from AttributionBench
\cite{li2024attributionbench}, each sampled by hash (Table~\ref{tab:props}). Every source was
reconstructed exactly, and a second run gave identical identifiers. After one random character was
inserted into each source, every old identifier resolved to \texttt{stale\_id}, whereas more than half
of the raw character ranges, and about a third of position-only identifiers in Thai, would have returned changed
text without any error. The cost is that identifiers into unchanged text are invalidated as well, so every
citation into an edited source must be selected again. Unit boundaries fell inside a word in under 0.5\%
of cuts, against 75\% (Thai) and 65\% (English) for fixed chunks of the same mean length, and never
between two digits. Segmentation took about 0.19\,s per 1,000 Thai characters on a CPU. Word boundaries
come from a reference tokenizer, not from gold annotation.

\subsection{Warning-Layer Data and Metrics}
We use WiCE test (1,070 pairs) \cite{kamoi2023wice}, AttributionBench test (1,610 in-domain, 1,686
out-of-domain rows) \cite{li2024attributionbench} and its Google translation into Thai, and 20
AttributionBench clusters for the replay; all labels come from the benchmarks. FAR is the share of
gold-unsupported items labelled supported, the error that matters most for a warning label; FRR is the
share of supported items labelled unsupported. Intervals come from bootstrap resampling of whole
question/response clusters.

\subsection{Verifier Design on WiCE}

WiCE provides claim/evidence pairs from Wikipedia with entailment labels
\cite{kamoi2023wice}. Design choices (selector, threshold, decision head, backbone) were made on the
development split, and we report the held-out test split. We compare four rules: B0, the probability of
entailment on the full evidence with a development-tuned threshold; B1, the same rule on the
selected units; B1-LR, the deployed rule; and B3, which adds reverse-direction NLI
features to the logistic head. Uncertainty uses 1,000 paired bootstrap resamples.


Fig.~\ref{fig:results}a shows that selection alone raises supported recall but also raises false
acceptance; adding the logistic boundary recovers precision. B1-LR improves Macro-F1 over B0
by 0.033 (95\% CI 0.013--0.053) and supported F1 by 0.058 (0.029--0.087), with no significant
change in false acceptance ($+0.75$ pp, CI $-1.09$ to $+2.70$). Relative to B1, the logistic
boundary lowers false acceptance by 5.42 points (CI 3.70--7.17). B3 is statistically
indistinguishable from B1-LR (Macro-F1 $-0.002$, CI $-0.011$ to $+0.007$) at roughly twice the
latency, so B1-LR was kept. The absolute level is modest: about one in four gold-unsupported pairs is
still accepted. Other selectors, heads and backbones were rejected on development data.

\subsection{Transfer to Machine-Translated Thai}

We rescored the AttributionBench test sets with the frozen B1-LR in English and in Thai. All 33,583
unique claim and reference texts were translated English$\to$Thai with Google Cloud Translation (NMT
model), keeping row order, unit segmentation and gold labels. Both languages were rescored with
identical frozen assets on the same device. Thai labels are \emph{inherited} from English under a
label-invariance assumption; no bilingual human adjudication was performed. Context matters here:
the NLI checkpoint saw Thai only during pre-training, as Thai was held out of its NLI fine-tuning,
and its model card reports XNLI accuracy of 0.786 for Thai against 0.871 for English
\cite{laurer2022mdeberta}.


Fig.~\ref{fig:results}b shows why a single F1 value is misleading here. In-domain F1 is
unchanged (.634 in both languages), yet the Thai condition accepts 140 more unsupported rows and
recovers 65 more supported ones: the operating point shifts toward permissiveness. Out of domain,
Thai lowers F1 by 0.040 (CI 0.018--0.063) and raises false acceptance by 4.4 points. For a check whose
purpose is to warn about unsupported claims, the rise in false acceptance is the result that
matters: on Thai in-domain text, about half of unsupported claims would carry no warning.

More Thai pairs hit the 512-token limit (311 vs.\ 288 in-domain, 154 vs.\ 124 out-of-domain), and
BGE-M3 \cite{chen2024bgem3} similarity between each text and its translation (mean cosine 0.846) shows
topical closeness, not that names, amounts and negations survived; translation, language and truncation
effects are not separated.

\subsection{What If the Warning Were a Filter?}\label{sec:replay}

To see what the check changes downstream, we replayed the judge $M_J$ on AttributionBench
rather than on cases, because only there do claims carry gold labels. Twenty label-blind,
hash-ordered clusters (10 ID, 10 OOD; 60 rows, 29 attributable and 31 not) were each turned into a
virtual case whose claims are the benchmark claims and whose sources are their references,
segmented into units exactly as production does. The same claims, identifiers and prompt were sent
to $M_J$ under two conditions: \emph{no gate} (every claim admitted) and \emph{B1-LR gate}
(only \texttt{supported} claims admitted, using the frozen decisions). $M_J$ used Gemma~4~26B
with temperature 0, a thinking budget of 8,192 tokens and fixed providers without fallback. The
outcome is whether a non-attributable row's claim identifier is cited in the generated summary.


As a gate, B1-LR lowered the share of unsupported claim identifiers cited in summaries from
100\% to 22.2\% (paired difference $-77.8$ pp, cluster-bootstrap CI $-95.2$ to $-53.3$). Seven
cluster pairs lost unsupported citations and none gained them (exact McNemar $p=0.016$); the ID and
OOD halves are each too small to be conclusive alone ($p=0.125$ and $0.25$). The cost is visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:results}c: 9 of 29 supported claims were withheld, most of them in-domain, and four clusters
had no admitted claim at all, so no summary was produced.

\paragraph{Failures and an exposure.}
Six of the 36 calls to $M_J$ failed, three per condition, all by exhausting the output budget on model
reasoning; those pairs are reported as unknown and excluded, and no successful output cited a withheld
or unknown identifier. One gated summary added a figure found in no admitted claim but in the cited passages, so citation text
reaching $M_J$ is a path no claim-level check examines.

%======================================================================
\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{What the method guarantees, and what it does not.}
The construction guarantees that every displayed quotation exists in a stored source at the stated
range, that no identifier silently survives an edit, and that every summary sentence points only to
claims the analysis produced. It does not guarantee that a claim is supported by its spans, that the model cited all the
evidence it should have, or that the segmenter drew the most useful boundaries: a unit is the finest
granularity a citation can have, so a poor split makes citations coarser, though never wrong in
location. These questions are semantic, so they are left to the optional warning layer.

\paragraph{Gate or label.}
The replay shows the trade-off: a filter that removes about three quarters of unsupported claims from
summaries also removes about three in ten supported ones, and the Thai results suggest it would be
leakier on Thai input. A silently missing true fact can cost an investigator as much as a flagged dubious
one, and a filter hides its errors where a label shows them. The verdict is therefore displayed beside
the claim and nothing else changes, while the structural checks of Sect.~\ref{sec:binding} stay strict.

\paragraph{Threats to validity.}
These guarantees are argued from construction and checked on public text (Sect.~\ref{sec:props}),
but the method has not yet been compared head to head with a quote-writing pipeline, and ThaiSum is
edited news rather than complaints or OCR output. The replay measures whether unsupported claim identifiers are cited, not whether
the prose is faithful. Thai labels are inherited through machine translation, with translation, language
and truncation effects confounded. WiCE and AttributionBench are English web data, not complaints, so
we claim no accuracy on real cases and no benefit to investigators. A smaller MiniLM backbone tested
afterwards scored higher on WiCE (Macro-F1 .650 vs.\ .618), so the deployed backbone is not claimed
optimal.

\paragraph{Ethics and privacy.}
Complaints contain personal and financial data. OCR and LLM stages were called through external
APIs, so deployment on real complaints needs a data-processing agreement or self-hosted models. The
system does not decide guilt, and no real case data was used in these experiments.

%======================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%======================================================================

We showed how to make evidence traceability a property of an LLM case-analysis system rather than a
request to the model: cut every source into lossless units, name each unit by the exact text it came
from, let the model select short aliases but never write a quotation, and bind every selection
deterministically, so that each summary sentence leads to claims and each claim to a verbatim span with
its page. A frozen semantic check can sit on top as a warning at about 0.23\,s per claim on a consumer
GPU, but our measurements show why it should warn rather than decide. Next, a proof of concept will
run the same public Thai and English cases through a quote-writing pipeline and through this method,
comparing quotation fidelity, pointer validity, coverage and output tokens without human labels; the
warning layer will be tested on the Thai XNLI test set \cite{conneau2018xnli} and a small doubly annotated
subset of real, anonymised complaints.
